The manuscript addresses an important and clinically relevant problem: accurate diabetic-retinopathy grading with limited labelled data. The integration of heterogeneous teacher networks, knowledge distillation, multi-temperature supervision, and ordinal-aware learning is promising, and the manuscript is generally well organized. However, several methodological inconsistencies and reporting issues must be resolved before the reliability of the conclusions can be assessed.

1. Correct the inconsistency in the QWK results. The manuscript states that equal-weight fusion and entropy-gated fusion produced identical predicted labels for all 367 test images, but Table 2 reports different QWK values of 0.902 and 0.907. Identical predictions evaluated against the same ground-truth labels must produce the same QWK; therefore, the authors should recheck the predictions, metric implementation, table values, and bootstrap analysis.

2. Clarify whether the test set influenced model selection. Section II states that the test set was withheld from all model-development decisions, but Table 1 compares nine architectures on the test set, and the discussion appears to use their test QWK values to justify architecture selection. The authors should explain the exact selection procedure and ensure that all architecture, loss-weight, fusion, and hyperparameter decisions were based only on training and validation data.

3. Separate student-only and full-system performance. Equation (8) indicates that the principal prediction is obtained by averaging the teacher-ensemble and student logits at inference, whereas the reported latency of 6.70 ms/image belongs only to the ConvNeXt Tiny student. The authors should report accuracy, macro-F1, QWK, AUC, parameter count, memory consumption, and latency separately for the student-only model and the complete teacher–student inference system.

4. Provide a controlled ablation of all proposed components. Table 3 changes the student architecture and other components simultaneously, so it does not isolate the contributions of multi-temperature distillation, ordinal CDF loss, and non-inferiority loss. A controlled ablation using the same ConvNeXt Tiny backbone should compare weighted CE alone, CE plus single-temperature KD, CE plus multi-temperature KD, CE+KD+ordinal loss, CE+KD+non-inferiority loss, and the complete objective.

5. Reconsider the conclusion regarding superiority over standalone models. Table 1 reports QWK = 0.923 for the custom Fusion CNN–ViT model, which is higher than the proposed system’s mean QWK of 0.9186. Therefore, the statement that the proposed method consistently improves over standalone backbones is not supported by the presented results and should either be revised or demonstrated through an identical multi-seed experimental protocol.

6. Demonstrate the actual benefit of using Teacher

1. Teacher 1 obtains QWK = 0.640, considerably below Teacher 2’s QWK of 0.901, while equal-weight fusion improves QWK by only 0.001. The authors should compare Teacher-2-only distillation with two-teacher distillation and report disagreement rate, error correlation, KL divergence, or another diversity measure to demonstrate that Teacher 1 contributes useful complementary information.

7. Justify the averaging of raw logits. The two teachers have different architectures and may produce logits with different numerical scales, making direct equal-weight logit averaging potentially unreliable. The authors should compare raw-logit averaging with probability averaging and temperature-calibrated logit averaging, or provide evidence that the output scales are sufficiently compatible.

8. Support the claims concerning calibration. The manuscript states that spectral normalization improves calibration and produces better-calibrated teacher logits, but no calibration experiment is presented. Confidence alone does not establish calibration; the authors should report metrics such as expected calibration error, Brier score, negative log-likelihood, and, if possible, reliability diagrams.

9. Explain the preprocessing used with pretrained models. ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50, ViT-B/16, and ConvNeXt Tiny models are used without ImageNet normalization, which differs from their usual pretrained input protocol. The authors should justify this choice and preferably provide an ablation comparing raw input scaling with ImageNet normalization and an appropriate retinal-image preprocessing method.

10. Provide direct evidence for the low-data claim. Although APTOS is relatively small, the experiments use the complete available training partition, and no reduced-data experiment is reported. Since “low-data” is central to the title and motivation, the authors should evaluate the method using controlled fractions such as 10%, 25%, 50%, and 100% of the training data or moderate the low-data claims.

11. Clarify the non-inferiority loss. In Equation (5), the positive margin requires the student loss to be lower than the teacher loss by a specified amount, which appears closer to a superiority-margin hinge loss than conventional non-inferiority. The authors should justify the terminology and sign of the margin, explain whether teacher losses are detached from gradient computation, and provide an ablation demonstrating the contribution of this term.

12. Report complete reproducibility details. The optimization protocol is mainly described for the ConvNeXt Tiny student, while the training procedures for Teacher 1, Teacher 2, the gated model, and the standalone baselines remain incomplete. Please provide the optimizer, learning rate, batch size, epoch limit, initialization, backbone-freezing strategy, checkpoint-selection rule, software versions, hardware specifications, and teacher-training seeds for every major configuration.

13. Correct and expand the computational-efficiency analysis. Based on the values in Table 5, the full system should contain approximately 149.8M parameters (8.2M + 113.8M + 27.8M), not 142.0M. The authors should correct this calculation and report latency using the same hardware, batch size, precision, warm-up procedure, and number of repetitions for all models, including the complete system.

14. Strengthen the statistical evaluation. Three seeds provide only limited evidence of stability, particularly with a test set of 367 images and very small differences between configurations. The authors should report confidence intervals or paired bootstrap tests for the principal model comparisons and explain the bootstrap sampling unit, number of resamples, and random seed.

15. Provide a more clinically informative class-wise analysis. Figure 2 shows only 5 correct predictions among 19 severe cases, indicating weak performance for an important minority class despite the high overall QWK. Please report per-class precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, class-wise sample counts, and clinically relevant referable-DR sensitivity and specificity, together with confidence intervals.

16. Clarify Figure 2 and the reported final results. The confusion matrix is labelled “ConvNeXtTiny_Residual,” but it is unclear whether it represents the standalone student or the final teacher–student logit-averaging system. This distinction should be stated explicitly in the figure caption, and confusion matrices for both inference modes would make the deployment trade-off clearer.

17. Improve the comparison with published studies. The authors appropriately acknowledge that different studies use different splits and evaluation protocols, but stronger care is needed because some reported kappa values may not be quadratic weighted kappa. Dataset split, augmentation, validation method, kappa definition, and whether each value is from a single run or cross-validation should be included wherever the information is available.

18. Check the references for relevance and consistency. The reference list is substantial and reasonably current, but all bibliographic details, publication status, page numbers, volumes, issues, DOI information, and access dates should be verified. The authors should also ensure that each citation directly supports the associated claim, particularly claims about calibration, ordinal learning, and multi-teacher diversity.

19. Moderate the practical and clinical deployment claims. The manuscript has been evaluated only on APTOS 2019, without external validation, prospective testing, demographic fairness analysis, or device-specific evaluation. Statements concerning screening deployment, regulated workflows, and on-device use should therefore be presented as potential future applications rather than demonstrated capabilities.

20. Revise the AI-use declaration. The statement first says that artificial intelligence was not used and then states that Claude was used for grammar checking, which is internally inconsistent. It should clearly state that no generative AI was used for scientific content, experimental analysis, or result generation, while Claude was used only for language or grammar correction, subject to the journal’s disclosure policy.

21. Address presentation and formatting issues. Table 5 appears after the beginning of the RQ1 discussion even though it belongs to the preceding computational-efficiency subsection, and some headings and line numbers are crowded. Metric notation, decimal precision, mean ± standard deviation reporting, table placement, equation typography, and the distinction between single-run and multi-seed results should be made consistent throughout. Overall, the study has potential and addresses a meaningful application. Nevertheless, the contradictory QWK values, uncertainty regarding test-set independence, insufficient component-wise ablation, and mixing of full-system performance with student-only efficiency are major issues. The manuscript may be reconsidered after these points are addressed through corrected calculations, clearer reporting, and additional controlled experiments.

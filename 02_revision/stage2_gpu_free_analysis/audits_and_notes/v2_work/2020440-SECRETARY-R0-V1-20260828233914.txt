
Hybrid teacher ensemble knowledge distillation with ordinal-aware loss for low-data diabetic retinopathy grading
Abstract
Context—Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most prevalent microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and one of the leading causes of preventable blindness worldwide. Population-level screening relies on fundus photography, but manual grading by ophthalmologists is time-intensive, costly and subject to inter-grader variability. Automated deep learning graders can relieve this burden, but two obstacles persist: expert-annotated fundus datasets remain small, so models must be trained in a low-data regime, and the five-level International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy scale is ordinal, whereas conventional cross-entropy training treats every misgrading as equally costly and is thus misaligned with the quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) metric.
Objective—The aim of this study is to develop and evaluate ConvNeXtTiny_Residual, a teacher–student knowledge distillation framework that jointly addresses low-data training and ordinal grade structure in five-class DR grading, and to determine whether a diverse teacher ensemble, multi-temperature distillation and an ordinal-aware objective improve QWK without a learned fusion module.
Method—Two structurally diverse CNN–ViT hybrid teachers were trained independently — a lightweight confidence-gated baseline hybrid and an advanced ResNet50 + ViT-B/16 model with a spectral-normalized head — and their logits combined by equal-weight averaging. A compact ConvNeXt Tiny student was trained on the resulting soft targets with a four-component objective comprising class-weighted cross-entropy, multi-temperature Kullback–Leibler distillation at T ∈ {2.0, 4.0}, an ordinal cumulative distribution function loss aligned with QWK, and a non-inferiority loss with a scheduled margin. Experiments used APTOS 2019 (3,662 fundus images) with a stratified 80/10/10 split, 224 × 224 inputs, no augmentation, AdamW optimization, early stopping on validation QWK and three seeds; teacher and student logits were averaged directly at inference.

Results—The proposed system achieved a mean test QWK of 0.9186 ± 0.0010 and a mean accuracy of 85.47% ± 0.31%. Replacing the lightweight student backbone with ConvNeXt Tiny produced the largest single gain (QWK +0.0031, accuracy +0.91 percentage points). The equal-weight teacher ensemble reached a test QWK of 0.902 against 0.901 for the stronger single teacher, whereas an entropy-gated alternative collapsed (mean gate ≈ 0.09), returned identical labels for all 367 test images and gave a QWK difference that was not statistically robust (+0.005; bootstrap 95% CI [−0.002, 0.012]). The 27.8M-parameter student ran at 6.70 ± 0.01 ms per image, with errors concentrated between adjacent grades.
Conclusion—Structured multi-teacher distillation combined with ordinal-aware optimization can improve five-class DR grading under limited-data conditions while keeping inference simple, and equal-weight fusion is sufficient when the teachers are confident and largely agree. Because the evaluation uses a single dataset and three seeds, external validation on EyePACS, Messidor and DDR, together with calibration and fairness analysis, remains necessary before screening deployment.
Key Words—Diabetic retinopathy grading, Knowledge distillation, CNN–ViT hybrid, Ordinal CDF loss, Teacher ensemble, APTOS 2019

I. INTRODUCTION
Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most prevalent microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and one of the leading causes of preventable vision loss and blindness worldwide [1]. The International Diabetes Federation reports that approximately 537 million adults were living with diabetes in 2021, a figure projected to rise to 783 million by 2045 [2]. Routine fundus photography-based screening is therefore considered essential for preventing avoidable blindness at the population level.
Conventional screening relies on ophthalmologists who manually examine fundus images and assign a severity grade on standardized clinical scales. The International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) scale defines five ordinal grades, from no apparent retinopathy (Grade 0) to proliferative DR (Grade 4) [3]. Manual grading is nevertheless time-intensive, costly, and subject to inter-grader variability, which complicates the establishment of reliable reference standards for evaluating automated systems [4] and motivates accurate, computationally efficient automation.
Deep learning (DL) has transformed automated DR grading. Landmark studies by Gulshan et al. [5] and Ting et al. [6] demonstrated that deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs) could match ophthalmologists in detecting referable DR from fundus photographs, and subsequent research explored progressively deeper architectures—ResNet [7], EfficientNet [8], DenseNet [9]—achieving substantial gains on EyePACS and APTOS 2019; related studies examined DR detection across geographic and ethnic variability [10] and transfer learning under limited-data constraints [11]. The survey by Zhu et al. [12], covering 94 studies from 2018 to 2023, confirmed that ResNet and VGGNet remain the dominant frameworks, with APTOS 2019 and EyePACS the most widely adopted benchmarks. Two fundamental challenges nevertheless remain unresolved.
The first is the scarcity of labeled training data. APTOS 2019, one of the largest publicly available graded fundus datasets, contains only 3,662 labeled images—orders of magnitude fewer than the datasets used to pretrain general-purpose vision models—so many DL systems must be trained in a low-data regime where overfitting is likely. The second is the ordinal nature of the grading scale. Severity grades follow a monotonic ordering (0 < 1 < 2 < 3 < 4) in which adjacent grades share clinical similarity, yet standard cross-entropy treats all misclassifications equally and cannot encode the cost difference between confusing Grade 0 with Grade 1 and with Grade 4. This misalignment with the quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) metric—which weights errors by grade distance—is a structural limitation that recent surveys continue to identify [13].
In parallel, Vision Transformers (ViT) [14] have shown that self-attention can model long-range spatial dependencies, capturing global structure that locally constrained convolutions may miss, and systematic comparisons report that hybrid CNN–ViT models often outperform either architecture in isolation on DR grading [15]. Knowledge distillation (KD) offers a complementary answer to the low-data challenge: a compact student is trained not only on hard labels but also on the soft probability distributions of a powerful pre-trained teacher, inheriting its richer representations [16]. Islam et al. [17], for example, fused a ResNet152V2 and a Swin Transformer to distil knowledge into a lightweight Xception student, achieving high multi-class accuracy on APTOS 2019 while remaining deployable in resource-constrained settings.
Despite these advances, several research gaps remain. First, structurally diverse CNN–ViT hybrid teachers have not been sufficiently examined within low-data KD pipelines for five-class DR grading, particularly when the student must stay compact enough for practical screening deployment. Second, most KD approaches rely on a single teacher or a single distillation temperature, which may limit the transfer of complementary supervisory signals across severity levels. Third, the ordinal structure of the grading scale is rarely embedded directly into the distillation objective, even though QWK penalizes errors by grade distance. Finally, the added complexity of adaptive or gated fusion may not be justified in low-data settings unless it clearly outperforms equal-weight teacher fusion.
This paper proposes ConvNeXtTiny_Residual, a teacher–student distillation framework designed for five-class DR grading under low-data conditions. An ensemble of two structurally distinct, independently trained CNN–ViT hybrid teachers is combined by equal-weight fusion to provide the student with a diverse, well-calibrated knowledge signal, and a compact ConvNeXt Tiny [18] student is trained with a multi-component loss integrating weighted cross-entropy, multi-temperature distillation, an ordinal cumulative distribution function (CDF) loss aligned with QWK, and a non-inferiority constraint with a scheduled margin.
The study is guided by the following research questions:
(1) RQ1: Does the proposed hybrid teacher–student framework improve diabetic retinopathy grading performance compared to baseline backbones and existing DR grading approaches?
(2) RQ2: Does the proposed ordinal-aware distillation strategy provide a measurable contribution to performance, particularly in terms of the quadratic weighted kappa (QWK)?
(3) RQ3: Is equal-weight fusion a sufficient and more practical ensemble strategy than entropy-gated fusion under low-data conditions?
The principal contributions of this work are:
(1) A hybrid teacher ensemble combining a custom baseline CNN–ViT hybrid and an advanced ResNet50+ViT-B/16 model with spectral normalization via equal-weight logit averaging.
(2) A multi-temperature distillation scheme at T ∈ {2.0, 4.0} with fixed weighting, enabling simultaneous transfer of fine-grained discriminative and coarse semantic knowledge across DR severity grades.
(3) An ordinal CDF loss that penalizes deviations from the correct cumulative grade distribution, aligning the training objective with QWK and the clinically motivated ordinal structure of DR grading.
(4) A non-inferiority loss with scheduled margin enforcing sample-wise parity between the student and the teacher ensemble throughout training.
(5) Evaluation on APTOS 2019 reporting mean test QWK of 0.9186 ± 0.0010 and accuracy of 85.47% ± 0.31% across three random seeds.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section I.A reviews related work on deep learning-based DR grading, CNN–ViT hybrids, knowledge distillation, and ordinal-aware learning. Section II describes the proposed teacher–student framework and the experimental protocol. Section III presents the evaluation results, baseline comparisons and ablation study on APTOS 2019, and discusses them with respect to the research questions. Section IV concludes with practical implications, limitations, and future work.
A. Related work
The following subsections review deep learning for DR grading, CNN–ViT hybrid architectures, knowledge distillation for medical images, and multi-teacher ensembles.
1. Deep learning for diabetic retinopathy grading
Deep learning for DR grading has evolved rapidly since Gulshan et al. [5] and Ting et al. [6] established that CNNs could approach ophthalmologist-level accuracy at population scale. The review by Zhu et al. [12] confirms that transfer learning with ResNet and VGGNet backbones is the dominant paradigm. On the APTOS 2019 five-class task, Farag et al. [19] augmented a DenseNet169 encoder with a Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM), reaching 82% accuracy and QWK = 0.888 using only cross-entropy, while Shakibania et al. [20] combined EfficientNet-B0 and ResNet50 as parallel feature extractors and reported QWK = 0.93 with 89.6% accuracy, improving on prior single-backbone results for both binary and multi-class grading.
Recent work has increasingly focused on enriching feature representations. Tian et al. [21] proposed FA+KC-Net, combining fine-grained attention with lesion knowledge extraction and achieving top-tier performance across APTOS 2019, DDR, Messidor, and EyePACS. Romero-Oraá et al. [22] separated attention on dark and bright retinal structures—hemorrhages and exudates respectively—improving both accuracy and visual interpretability for clinicians. Nevertheless, recent surveys still highlight unresolved challenges in robustness, interpretability, and clinically deployable model design [13], which motivate the ordinal-aware training objective proposed here.
A persistent structural challenge is severe class imbalance. The APTOS 2019 dataset [23] provides 3,662 labeled fundus images from clinics in India across all five severity grades, in which mild and moderate cases substantially outnumber severe and proliferative ones; it has become one of the most widely adopted benchmarks for evaluating DR grading under realistic imbalance.
2. CNN–ViT hybrid architectures for diabetic retinopathy
The Vision Transformer (ViT), introduced by Dosovitskiy et al. [14], showed that splitting an image into fixed-size patches and processing the resulting sequence with multi-head self-attention can match leading CNNs on standard classification benchmarks. Its key advantage is the ability to model global spatial dependencies, associating distant retinal regions—such as vascular patterns near the optic disc and macular lesions—that jointly indicate DR severity.
The data efficiency limitations of pure ViT architectures motivated hybrid CNN–ViT models. Zhang et al. [15] evaluated CNN, ViT, and hybrid architectures on DR classification and found hybrids to perform favorably: their CvT-13 achieved QWK = 0.84 and AUC = 0.93, and their Grad-CAM and Attention-Rollout analysis indicated that CNNs focus on fine-grained lesion textures whereas ViTs show broader contextual attention. Ikram and Imran [24] proposed ResViT FusionNet, integrating ResNet50 with ViT branches and reaching kappa = 0.8935 and 93.01% accuracy with LIME and Grad-CAM explainability.
For retinal imaging beyond DR, Niranjan et al. [25] introduced a hybrid ViT–GNN architecture on APTOS 2019 with 93.2% accuracy and AUC = 0.961, and Nazih et al. [26] showed that a ViT optimized with AdamW and focal loss reaches an F1-score of 0.825 and AUC of 0.956 on DR severity grading. A comparative study by Goh et al. [27] across large retinal image collections further reported that ViTs outperform CNNs for detecting referable DR.
In this work, teacher diversity is supported quantitatively in Section III.B (Table 2). We interpret it as complementary predictive behavior in soft-label space; no post-hoc saliency or attention-visualization experiments were run in our pipeline.
3. Knowledge distillation for medical image classification
Knowledge distillation, first systematically formulated by Hinton et al. [16], trains a compact student by combining cross-entropy on hard labels with a Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the temperature-softened distributions of teacher and student. Soft teacher probabilities encode inter-class similarity structure—“dark knowledge”—that one-hot labels lack; Gou et al. [28] survey the resulting variants.
For DR grading, Luo et al. [29] proposed a self-KD framework with CAM-attention, achieving AUC = 0.965 and 92.9% accuracy on Messidor without an external teacher. Serener and Serte [10] studied DR detection with geographic and ethnic information, and Patil et al. [11] investigated hyperparameter transfer learning. Ju et al. [30] combined KD with multi-task learning across DR and AMD labels, improving DR grading accuracy by 5.91%; Moya-Albor et al. [31] used an Inception-v3 teacher–student pipeline with a combined KL-divergence and cross-entropy loss, reaching 99% training and 97% validation accuracy on lesion classification; and Wang et al. [32] focused knowledge transfer on lesion regions through a global lesion embedding queue.
Islam et al. [17] showed that a multi-teacher fusion of ResNet152V2 and a Swin Transformer distilled into a lightweight Xception student outperforms single-teacher approaches, and Chilukoti et al. [33] transferred knowledge from a MobileViTv2 teacher to a GoogLeNet student on APTOS, reaching a weighted kappa of 0.90.
A critical limitation of single-temperature KD is its inability to transfer coarse semantic and fine-grained discriminative knowledge simultaneously. The present work addresses this with multi-temperature distillation at T ∈ {2.0, 4.0}, following the established insight [16],[28] that higher temperatures yield more uniform soft distributions suited to coarse transfer, whereas lower temperatures preserve the sharper peaks needed to separate adjacent severity grades.
The ordinal structure of DR grading further motivates specialized loss formulations. Cao et al. [34] proposed CORAL, reformulating ordinal regression as binary sub-problems with shared representations to ensure rank consistency, and Liu et al. [35] introduced unimodal regularization that concentrates probability mass near the true class. We instead adopt an ordinal CDF loss that penalizes deviations between predicted and target cumulative distribution functions, providing a differentiable regularizer explicitly aligned with QWK.
4. Teacher ensemble and multi-teacher distillation
Ensemble learning improves predictive accuracy by aggregating diverse model predictions, and in the KD context a teacher ensemble provides the student with a richer soft-label signal. Yilmaz and Aiyengar [36] showed that cross-architecture KD from a ViT teacher to a CNN student reaches 89% accuracy on fundus images with 97.4% fewer parameters, retaining about 93% of the teacher's diagnostic performance, and Islam et al. [17] reported that a ResNet152V2+Swin teacher ensemble outperforms either teacher alone.
Most multi-teacher approaches aggregate teacher predictions by simple averaging or confidence weighting. We investigated an entropy-gated alternative but found that, in the low-data setting with a high-confidence teacher ensemble (mean teacher confidence ≈ 0.88), the gate collapsed to near-zero student weighting (mean gate value ≈ 0.09) and yielded results statistically indistinguishable from equal-weight averaging. Equal-weight fusion was therefore adopted, consistent with the finding that gating mechanisms require sufficient inter-teacher uncertainty to be beneficial [28].
II. METHOD
This section describes the proposed teacher–student framework: the two hybrid teacher networks and their equal-weight fusion, the ConvNeXt Tiny student, the four-component training objective, the inference strategy, and the dataset and training protocol used in all experiments.
A. Overview of the proposed framework
The proposed framework, ConvNeXtTiny_Residual, is a teacher–student knowledge distillation system for five-class DR severity grading under low-data conditions; Figure 1 illustrates the pipeline. Given a fundus image of size 224 × 224 × 3, two independently trained CNN–ViT hybrid teachers produce logits that are combined by equal-weight arithmetic averaging into zt, while a ConvNeXt Tiny student [18] processes the same input to produce zs. During training the student is optimized with a four-component loss combining weighted cross-entropy, multi-temperature distillation, ordinal CDF regularization, and a non-inferiority constraint. At inference the reported full-system configuration uses no learned gate or extra fusion parameter: final logits are (zt + zs)/2, as given in Eq. (8).

Figure 1. Overview of the proposed ConvNeXtTiny_Residual pipeline. Two CNN–ViT hybrid teachers produce an equal-weight teacher logit signal; the ConvNeXt Tiny student is trained with a four-component loss and, at inference, its logits are averaged with the teacher logits.
B. Teacher network 1: baseline CNN–ViT hybrid
The first teacher is a dual-branch architecture combining CNN and ViT representations through a confidence-gated fusion mechanism.
CNN branch. The CNN branch uses a Conv–BatchNorm–ReLU stem followed by three residual-style stages that progressively increase channel dimensionality from 64 to 256. The resulting feature map is globally average-pooled and projected through a fully connected layer to produce the CNN feature vector fcnn ∈ ℝ512.
ViT branch. The ViT branch tokenizes the input with a 16 × 16 patch embedding convolution, producing 196 tokens of dimension 256. Six transformer blocks with 8-head self-attention and MLP layers encode the sequence; token-wise mean pooling followed by linear projection yields fvit ∈ ℝ512.
Confidence-gated fusion. Scalar confidence scores are computed as sc = σ(Wcfcnn) and st = σ(Wtfvit), and a two-layer MLP estimates the fusion gate given in Eq. (1):
α = σMLPsc, st,   ffused = αfcnn + 1 - αfvit
(1)

C. Teacher network 2: advanced CNN–ViT hybrid with spectral normalization
The second teacher employs larger-scale pretrained backbones to maximize representational capacity. A pretrained ResNet50 encodes the input and global average pooling reduces its output to a 2048-dimensional vector, while a pretrained ViT-B/16 encoder provides a 768-dimensional [CLS] representation of the same input. The two branches are concatenated into a 2816-dimensional joint feature, which a spectral-normalized linear head [37] maps to five logits zadv. Spectral normalization constrains the Lipschitz constant of the output layer, smoothing the decision boundary and improving calibration—a property critical for the near-boundary confusion cases prevalent in DR grading.
D. Teacher ensemble via equal-weight fusion
The composite teacher signal is formed by equal-weight averaging of the two teacher logits, as given in Eq. (2):
zt = 12zbase + zadv,   pteacher = softmaxzt
(2)

This equal-weight teacher ensemble is used during distillation to provide the soft teacher target. No learned teacher-level gate is used in the final reported equal-weight configuration.
E. Student network: ConvNeXt Tiny
A key design decision is the student backbone capacity. The lightweight CNN–ViT architectures explored in the ablation study are parameter-efficient but empirically insufficient to exploit the multi-teacher soft targets on the five-class ordinal task. We therefore select ConvNeXt Tiny [18]: a purely convolutional architecture with depthwise convolutions, inverted bottleneck blocks, and GELU activations—design choices inspired by ViT principles while preserving the inductive biases and inference efficiency of CNNs. The backbone is initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights and its classification head is replaced with a linear layer (768 → 5) producing zs.
F. Multi-component training loss
The student training objective addresses four distinct failure modes of standard distillation applied to ordinal medical grading: (1) class imbalance biasing predictions toward majority grades, (2) single-temperature KL-divergence failing to transfer both fine-grained and coarse knowledge simultaneously, (3) training–evaluation misalignment between cross-entropy and QWK, and (4) student regression below teacher performance during early training. The composite objective is given in Eq. (3):
L = Lmain + λniLni + λdistillLdistill + λordLord
(3)

1. Weighted cross-entropy loss
To address class imbalance, the primary classification loss is a class-weighted cross-entropy, defined in Eq. (4). No weighted random sampler or minority-class oversampling is used in the student runs; imbalance correction is applied directly inside the loss:
Lmain = -c=04wcyc log pc,   w = 0.40, 1.97, 0.73, 3.77, 2.49
(4)

where yc is the one-hot ground-truth indicator, p̂c = softmax(zs)c, and wc is the inverse-frequency class weight passed to CrossEntropyLoss(weight=class_w) for grades 0–4.
2. Non-inferiority loss
To prevent the student from performing worse than the teacher on individual training samples, the non-inferiority term of Eq. (5) is used:
Lni = 1Bi=1Bmax0, lsi - lti + me
(5)

where B is the mini-batch size, ls(i) and lt(i) are the per-sample cross-entropy losses of the student and teacher ensemble, respectively, and m(e) is a margin scheduled linearly over the epoch index e, decreasing from 0.010 at e = 1 to 0.003 at e = 70. The weighting coefficient follows the schedule λni: 0.80 → 0.25.
3. Multi-temperature distillation loss
The distillation loss of Eq. (6) is computed as a weighted combination of KL-divergence losses at two temperatures:
Ldistill = k ∈ 1, 2wkTk2 KLpteacherTksoftmaxzsTk
(6)

where pteacher(T_k) = softmax(zt/Tk) denotes the temperature-scaled teacher ensemble distribution. We use T1 = 2.0 (w1 = 0.6) and T2 = 4.0 (w2 = 0.4). The Tk2 scaling ensures gradient magnitude consistency across temperatures [16]. The overall distillation weight is λdistill = 0.30.
4. Ordinal CDF loss
DR severity grades exhibit a natural ordinal structure (0 < 1 < 2 < 3 < 4) that standard cross-entropy and KL-divergence losses do not explicitly encode. The ordinal CDF loss of Eq. (7) penalizes the squared difference between the predicted and target cumulative distribution functions:
Lord = 14k=03Fk - Fk*2
(7)

where F̂k denotes the predicted cumulative probability up to threshold k, and Fk* = 1[y ≤ k] is the corresponding target CDF for ground-truth grade y. The ordinal weight is λord = 0.12.
G. Inference strategy
The full-system inference configuration used for the main reported results deliberately avoids any learned gate, residual mixer, conflict brake, or additional fusion network. The teacher and student logits are combined by direct arithmetic averaging, as given in Eq. (8):
zfinal = 12zt + zs,   pfinal = softmaxzfinal,   y = arg maxcpfinalc
(8)

When computational constraints preclude running the teacher networks, the student-only prediction softmax(zs) can be used as a compact deployment mode. Section III therefore reports both standalone student behavior and full-system performance where relevant.
H. Experimental setup
This subsection describes the dataset and evaluation protocol, the preprocessing and augmentation settings, the optimization schedule, and the loss weights used for all reported runs.
1. Dataset and evaluation protocol
Dataset. All experiments use the APTOS 2019 Blindness Detection dataset [23], which comprises 3,662 fundus photographs annotated on the ICDR five-class scale: No DR (Grade 0, n = 1,805, 49.28%), Mild (Grade 1, n = 370, 10.10%), Moderate (Grade 2, n = 999, 27.28%), Severe (Grade 3, n = 193, 5.27%), and Proliferative DR (Grade 4, n = 295, 8.06%). Grades 0 and 2 account for approximately 76% of all samples while Grades 3 and 4 together comprise approximately 13%.
Data partitioning. The full dataset is partitioned using stratified random splitting into training (80%, n = 2,929), validation (10%, n = 366), and test (10%, n = 367) subsets. The validation set is used exclusively for early stopping and model selection; the test set is withheld entirely from all model development decisions and evaluated only once per configuration.
Evaluation metrics. The primary evaluation metric is QWK; secondary metrics include overall accuracy, macro-averaged F1-score, and ROC-AUC (one-vs-rest).
2. Data preprocessing and augmentation protocol
All experiments were conducted using retinal fundus images resized to 224 × 224 resolution through bilinear interpolation. Images were loaded in RGB format using the Python Imaging Library (PIL), converted into NumPy arrays, and subsequently transformed into PyTorch tensors for model training.
The preprocessing pipeline preserved the original image intensity distribution without applying ImageNet-style normalization statistics. As a result, the model received raw float-valued retinal image tensors during optimization.
To evaluate the proposed teacher–student framework under a controlled low-data setting, training was performed using a zero-augmentation protocol. No geometric or photometric augmentation operations were introduced during optimization, allowing the experimental results to primarily reflect the contribution of the proposed distillation strategy and optimization objective.
3. Optimization protocol
The ConvNeXt Tiny student backbone was optimized using AdamW with learning rate 2 × 10−4 and weight decay 1 × 10−4. All experiments were performed using batch size = 16. Learning rate scheduling was performed using CosineAnnealingWarmRestarts with parameters T0 = 10 and Tmult = 2. No warmup stage was employed during training.
Training was configured for at most 70 epochs with early stopping on validation QWK (patience = 10). Convergence occurred substantially earlier depending on the seed: the seed-42 run terminated at epoch 20 and the seed-52 run at epoch 14. All final experiments were repeated across seeds 42, 52, and 62, and metrics are reported as mean ± standard deviation.
4. Loss coefficients of the four-component objective
The global weights of the objective in Eq. (3) were selected on validation QWK. During the LiteCNNViT development stage they were set to (λdistill, λord) = (0.35, 0.15); after transitioning to the ConvNeXt Tiny student, validation experiments indicated that (0.30, 0.12) gave the most stable behavior, and these values were fixed for all three final seed runs. The non-inferiority coefficient decreased linearly from 0.80 to 0.25 and its margin from 0.010 to 0.003, while distillation temperatures and weights were kept at T ∈ {2.0, 4.0} and [0.6, 0.4]. The test set was not used in this process.

Both stages are reported together because they determine the teacher pool of the proposed pipeline.

1. Role of Teacher 1 in the ensemble
Teacher 1 is the lightweight baseline hybrid (~8.2M parameters) with confidence-gated CNN–ViT fusion (Section II.B). Its standalone performance is clearly below Teacher 2 (test QWK = 0.640, accuracy 63.22%, Table 2), so it is not treated as an independently strong classifier; it is retained because its logit stream is non-identical to the advanced teacher and slightly changes the ensemble soft target.
Design and measured behavior. Teacher 1 combines a shallow CNN stem, a compact six-block ViT encoder (16 × 16 patches, 256-d tokens), and a learned gate over branch features. On the test set its errors are dominated by adjacent-grade confusions and weak severe/proliferative discrimination.
Limited ensemble role. Equal-weight fusion reaches test QWK = 0.902, only +0.001 over Teacher 2 alone at the same rounded accuracy (83.11%). Teacher 1 is therefore best interpreted as a weak but non-redundant source of soft-label variation rather than a major performance driver—a role consistent with multi-teacher distillation as a regularizing signal, with the main predictive structure supplied by Teacher 2 [28],[38].
2. Role of Teacher 2 in the ensemble
Teacher 2 is the high-capacity advanced hybrid selected in Stage 2 (Section III.A), in which pretrained ResNet50 and ViT-B/16 features are concatenated (2816-d) and mapped through a spectral-normalized five-class head (Section II.C). Spectral normalization matched the top Stage 2 candidate in test QWK (0.901) and was preferred for calibrated teacher logits [37].
Measured performance. Table 2 reports validation accuracy 82.29% and QWK = 0.882, and test accuracy 83.11%, QWK = 0.901, and AUC = 0.964—the best single-teacher result in our study. Per-class test recall is 98.3% (No DR), 84.0% (Moderate), 36.8% (Severe), and 60.0% (Proliferative), with 20% of proliferative cases predicted as Moderate and Mild recall limited to 48.6%. These figures derive from the held-out test confusion structure, not from saliency visualization.
Ensemble and distillation role. Teacher 2 provides the dominant hard-label and soft-label quality, so complementarity is described here through prediction disagreement and ensemble metrics rather than verified spatial attention differences. It supplies most of the sharp structure in pteacher = softmax(zt) used for multi-temperature KL distillation at T ∈ {2.0, 4.0}; the non-inferiority loss is evaluated against the full ensemble zt, and equal-weight averaging (zbase + zadv)/2 is the fusion rule in the reported configuration.
3. Equal-weight versus entropy-gated teacher fusion
We additionally trained an entropy-gated MLP (hidden size 64) to weight the two teacher logit streams. On the test set the gate collapsed (ḡ ≈ 0.09; 86% of samples with g < 0.20), with no meaningful variation across grades or seeds, and predicted labels were identical to equal-weight fusion for all 367 test samples. The gated configuration's QWK advantage (+0.005; 0.907 vs. 0.902) was not statistically significant (bootstrap 95% CI [−0.002, 0.012]). Equal-weight fusion was therefore adopted for RQ3 [28].

F. RQ1: effectiveness of the hybrid teacher–student framework
Our results indicate that the proposed pipeline yields consistent improvements over standalone backbones and the alternative teacher–student configurations of Sections III.A–III.C. The gains are consistent with prior work on structurally diverse CNN–ViT hybrids and multi-teacher distillation [15],[17],[27],[41], where different architectures yield non-redundant soft targets. Teacher complementarity here is established through Table 2 and student-side confusion analysis, not Grad-CAM or attention-rollout studies. Recent medical-image KD studies provide context but should be read cautiously, as their tasks and teacher designs differ from ours. Domain-adaptive multi-teacher distillation and hybrid data-efficient KD both support the value of complementary teacher supervision under limited data or domain shift [38],[42], while Teach-Former and RL-based multi-teacher weighting suggest that richer multimodal or learned-weight strategies may help when teacher pools are more heterogeneous than the two-teacher setting used here [43],[44].
G. RQ2: contribution of ordinal-aware distillation to QWK
The ablation of Section III.C suggests that the ordinal CDF regularizer yields measurable QWK improvements relative to configurations trained without ordinal alignment, consistent with ordinal-classification approaches such as CORAL and unimodal regularization that explicitly encode class ordering [34],[35]. Recent DR reviews emphasize that robust grading remains an open methodological challenge [12],[13]; an ordinal objective therefore helps address the training–evaluation mismatch between cross-entropy optimization and QWK-type clinical metrics. Related retinal-disease KD work likewise supports that distillation targets improve graded predictions when they preserve inter-class structure [29],[30].
Evidence beyond DR points the same way: Litvinov et al. show that ordinal losses reduce severe misclassifications in BI-RADS mammography [45], and transformer-based medical-image classification with an ordinal loss reports improved ordinal performance consistent with our CDF approach [46]. These sources are more directly relevant to the ordinal-loss component than general DR classification work, and thus provide the main external support for interpreting the QWK gains in Table 3.
H. RQ3: practicality of equal-weight versus gated fusion
RQ3 asked whether equal-weight teacher fusion is sufficient under low-data conditions relative to an entropy-gated alternative. Section III.B.3 indicates that learned gating gave no practical advantage: the +0.005 QWK difference was not statistically robust and the predicted labels were identical, so the ordering is better read as a small calibration-level variation than a meaningful accuracy improvement. The gate itself supports this reading, collapsing toward near-zero outputs without learning a sample-adaptive weighting rule—plausibly because the two-teacher system operated under high teacher confidence and limited validation data, leaving insufficient disagreement to exploit.
These findings agree with broader guidance that simple averaging remains a strong baseline when ensemble members are reasonably calibrated and largely agree [28]. Spectral normalization in Teacher 2 further supports stable teacher logits [37], reducing the need for a learned fusion module. Equal-weight averaging is therefore attractive for reproducibility and deployment: it adds no trainable parameters, avoids gate-specific tuning, and provides a fixed, auditable fusion rule relevant to regulated screening workflows.
This is not a general rejection of adaptive fusion. Learned weighting may help when teacher pools are larger, more heterogeneous, or exhibit stronger disagreement [17],[43],[44]. Our results suggest only that, for this two-teacher low-data APTOS setting, equal-weight fusion offers the better trade-off between performance, simplicity, and reproducibility—the same principle behind the inference-time teacher–student averaging used here.
IV. CONCLUSION
In this work we proposed ConvNeXtTiny_Residual, a hybrid teacher-ensemble knowledge distillation pipeline tailored for five-class diabetic retinopathy grading under low-data constraints. Empirical evaluations on APTOS 2019 suggest that structured multi-teacher distillation combined with ordinal-aware loss terms can yield consistent improvements in clinically relevant metrics. The full-system configuration achieved the most favorable QWK and accuracy among our evaluated configurations by combining the teacher ensemble with the ConvNeXt Tiny student, while the student-only branch remains available as a compact deployment mode.
The contributions are threefold. First, a hybrid teacher ensemble of a lightweight and an advanced CNN–ViT teacher produces a better-calibrated soft target than either alone, potentially improving student learning stability (Table 2). Second, multi-temperature distillation with an ordinal CDF regularizer aligns the objective with distance-weighted clinical metrics (QWK), which may reduce clinically costly large-grade errors. Third, a non-inferiority constraint and equal-weight logit fusion may reduce student regression risk and simplify deployment. These findings should be read within the limits of a single-dataset, three-seed evaluation; broader validation on EyePACS, Messidor, DDR and prospective cohorts remains necessary.
A. Practical implications
The implications span model development, screening workflows, and deployment constraints. Combining heterogeneous teachers provides complementary supervisory signals that a sufficiently capacious student absorbs, enabling compact models that approximate ensemble behavior. For screening, the student branch offers a favorable performance–latency trade-off (mean ~6.7 ms/image), permitting near-real-time batch processing and on-device deployment on modest hardware, while the full ensemble remains available when resources allow. Calibration-promoting components and multi-temperature distillation improve probability estimates, which matters when referral decisions depend on model confidence, and equal-weight averaging simplifies ensemble maintenance and auditing relative to learned gating.
B. Limitations
Several limitations temper generalizability. First, the evaluation is restricted to APTOS 2019 and three seeds; geographic, camera, and population biases could limit external validity. Second, teacher training used large pretrained backbones and substantial compute, so reproducing the ensemble may be costly even though the student is compact. Third, label noise and inter-grader variability—well documented in retinal datasets—were not modeled beyond stratified splitting, which may inflate apparent performance. Fourth, our analysis indicates gate collapse in this low-data regime, and more sophisticated gating or disagreement-based selection was not explored. Fifth, lesion-level supervision, student-side augmentation ablations, and prospective clinical validation remain open gaps. Sixth, post-hoc saliency methods were not used: teacher complementarity is supported by architectural design, confusion-matrix statistics, and ensemble metrics, while qualitative CNN–ViT focus patterns are discussed only via prior literature [15].
C. Future work
Future directions follow from these limitations. Immediate steps are multi-dataset external validation (EyePACS, Messidor, DDR) to quantify generalization across acquisition settings and populations, and prospective evaluation on clinical cohorts. Methodologically, adaptive gating conditioned on explicit inter-teacher disagreement, post-hoc calibration methods, and student-side augmentation should be tested as separate ablations, since the reported results use zero augmentation. Lesion-level supervision or multi-task formulations could improve interpretability, and post-hoc explainability analyses could characterize why the two teachers disagree. For deployment, compressed student variants and quantization-aware training would reduce memory and energy footprints, and fairness analyses across demographic subgroups and imaging devices are essential before use in screening programs.
